What a Small Business Website Needs to Win Customers
what-a-small-business-website-needs
Six things a small business website needs before it can bring in enquiries, and a quick way to see which ones are missing on yours.
---
Most people open your website with one question in mind. Are these the people I need, and how do I reach them? If the answer is not visible within a few seconds, they close the tab and open the next result in Google.

Here is what a small business website needs before you think about anything fancier. We put the points in order of importance, so if you only have time for the first three, start there.

## 1. One clear sentence about what you do and where

Not "Innovative solutions for your home" but "Roof repairs in Plovdiv and the surrounding area". A visitor should know from the first line whether they are in the right place.

A good first sentence answers three questions. What do you do, who do you do it for, and where? Under it you can add one line that sets you apart, such as "Inspection within 48 hours and a written quote with material prices." A dental practice in Sofia, a car service in Burgas or an accountant in Varna can each say who they are in one line, and that is enough.

## 2. A phone number that can be tapped

On a phone nobody retypes a number. Put it at the top of the page as a link that starts a call, and do the same for your email address.

Check that the number matches the one in your Google profile. If you have two numbers, say which one is for what. If you answer messages on Viber or WhatsApp, say so, because many people would rather write than call.

## 3. Your services, with a few words about each

List three to six services and give each one a sentence about what it includes. If you have approximate prices, show them. Price is the first thing people look for, and when it is missing many simply do not ask.

You do not need a full price list. Something like "From 40 euro", or "The check-up costs 30 euro and is deducted from the treatment", or "The price depends on the area and is usually between two figures you name" is enough to make a person feel safe asking. If one service is your main one, give it a page of its own with more detail. That also helps Google understand what exactly you offer.

## 4. Proof that you are real

Show photos of your own work, your team and your premises. Add your address and opening hours, and Google reviews if you have them. People buy from people, and stock photos do not help.

Before and after photos work well for repairs, beauty treatments, cleaning and gardening. A photo of the team or the owner with a sentence or two about who you are builds trust quickly. Real reviews with a name, ideally linking to the Google profile, are stronger than any slogan. Company details in the footer, such as the company name, registration number and address, reassure people, especially when they are going to pay in advance.

## 5. An enquiry form with few fields

A name, a phone number and one box that says "How can we help?" are enough. Every extra required field puts some people off.

After the form is sent, show a clear message such as "We received your enquiry and will call you within one working day." Then keep that promise. A fast reply often matters more than the price, because the person has usually written to several places at once.

## 6. It works on a phone

A large share of visits to a local business website come from a phone. Open your site on your own phone and try to call yourselves from it. If you have to pinch and zoom, something is wrong.

Watch the speed as well. On mobile data, heavy photos load slowly and the visitor leaves before seeing anything at all.

## What you do not need at the start

Many small business websites are cluttered with things nobody uses. A slider with five pictures that change on their own is one example, because most people see only the first picture. A news section whose latest item is three years old is another, and if you will not keep it up, it is better not to have it. Music or a video that starts by itself annoys people. A page about mission and vision full of general words also does little, because one sentence about who you are on the home page does more.

## Common questions

**Do I need a website if I have a Facebook page?** Facebook is useful, but it is not yours. The rules and the reach change without warning, and people who search in Google rarely end up there. A website is the place you control, and it is the easiest one for Google and AI assistants to read. The two work best together.

**How many pages do I need?** For most local businesses four to six pages are enough. That means a home page, services (or a separate page for each main service), an about page, contacts and the legal pages. It matters more that each page answers the customer's questions clearly than that there are many pages.

**How often should the site be updated?** Whenever something important changes, such as prices, opening hours, services or a phone number. Once a month it is worth going through the site as a customer would.
