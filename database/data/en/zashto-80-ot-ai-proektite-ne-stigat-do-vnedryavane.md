Why 80 Percent of AI Projects Fail and What It Means for You
why-80-percent-of-ai-projects-fail
About 80 percent of AI projects never reach deployment. What sets apart those that work, and how a small business can start small.
---
An AI demonstration always looks good. You ask, you get an answer, everybody nods. Then real business arrives, with incomplete data, customers who ask unexpected things and the question of who is responsible if a mistake costs money.

Dr Pavlin Dobrev, a director at Bosch Digital and a founder and board member of BASSCOM, spoke at Digital Community Day 2026 about whether the Bulgarian market is ready for AI agents. His main point is that the winner is not the most impressive demonstration but the system that can prove itself.

## What the numbers say

About 80 percent of AI projects do not reach deployment, according to AlixPartners in its State of Enterprise Technology 2026. AI adoption in enterprises with 10 or more employees is 8.55 percent in Bulgaria, against 19.95 percent on average in the EU, according to Eurostat 2025.

The second number covers larger companies. From the talk we have no such data for firms with fewer people. But the direction is clear. In Bulgaria AI is less deployed, and what is in a pilot often stays in a pilot.

The speaker's point is that a pilot is easy and scaling is hard. He also says the euphoria is over and the check of the economics begins. The winner is the provable system.

## What an agent is

Dobrev describes an agent with several parts, which are a goal, observation, reasoning and planning, memory, tools and action. It sounds complicated, but the idea is simple. It is a program that does not only answer but does work in steps. The key is feedback, because reliability comes from feedback. An agent that does not see the result of its actions cannot correct itself.

Another of his thoughts is that the biggest change is not that machines think faster, but that we give them time to do the work.

## Three rules for a safe pilot

The speaker proposes a scheme that works for a small business too.

1. **A specification.** Describe what exactly the system must do, what it must not do, and when we count it as done well. One page is enough.
2. **Minimal rights.** AI gets access only to what it needs, and not to your whole mailbox or your bank account.
3. **An isolated environment with a task log.** You should be able to see what was done, when and why.

The principle that ties them together is that agents prepare the evidence and a human approves the decision based on risk. Even shorter, if it does not pass the tests, it does not exist.

## What this looks like in a small business

You do not need an AI strategy. You need one concrete task where a mistake is cheap and a person checks the result. Take a draft reply to a Google review. AI writes it, and you read, edit and publish it yourself. That is how our review tool works too, because nothing is published automatically and AI is forbidden to invent discounts and promises. Take a draft of a blog text. AI suggests a structure and paragraphs, but a person checks the facts and numbers. Or take an assistant on the website that answers from your own content. Our assistant Kreo answers from the content of the site, and when a visitor wants a conversation, it passes their contact to the team.

In each of these cases there is a specification, meaning what it may and may not do, a check, where a person reviews, and an approval, where a person decides. The principle is the same. AI proposes and the person decides.

## What checking the economics means

When the euphoria passes, the question is whether the system is worth it. For a small business the calculation is simple and fits on a sheet of paper. How long does the task take today? How long does it take with AI, including checking the result? What does the tool cost per month? What does one mistake cost and how often does it happen?

If checking the result eats up the saved time, the task is not suited to automation. If a mistake is expensive, a person should approve every action. That is what the speaker calls a decision based on risk.

## What not to do

Do not let AI send messages to customers without a review. Do not trust numbers you cannot verify. Do not start with a big project for everything, because three small tasks, each with a success criterion, are better. And do not leave the system without a log, because if you cannot say what it did, you cannot fix it.

## A short checklist before you start

Can you state the task in one sentence? What is the worst that can happen, and what does it cost? Who checks the result and how often? And how will we know it works, whether by a count, a time or money?

If you cannot answer all four, the project is not yet ready for AI. Dobrev says it like this. We do not scale individual demonstrations. We scale the repeatable system behind them.
